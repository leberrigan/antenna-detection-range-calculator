Reading this chart

  • Tag antenna gain is derived, not guessed. A ~18 cm whip at 166.38 MHz is about 0.1 λ (λ≈1.80 m) — far short of a resonant quarter‑wave (~45 cm). Modelled as a short monopole, its radiation resistance is only Rr≈40π²(h/λ)²≈4 Ω, comparable to plausible internal/matching loss resistance, so a meaningful share of the already‑tiny 0.1 mW never leaves the antenna. Combined with free‑space short‑dipole directivity (~1.8 dBi) and an orientation/polarization derate for a tag riding on a moving bird, the working estimate is −6 dBi. This is still the single largest uncertainty in the model: ±3 dB here shifts every range value by roughly ×1.4 or ×0.7.
  • Ground type has a fairly small effect on this boresight cut — switch terrains above and most range values move by only a percent or two. Seawater and freshwater (highly reflective, low‑loss surfaces) push the range fractionally higher; desert (dry, lossier) pulls it fractionally lower; average ground, forest and grassland land within noise of one another at this antenna's boresight. The bigger terrain effects in a full NEC2 model show up off‑boresight and very near the ground, not in this cut.
  • The 6 dB SNR requirement is fixed, not assumed — it's the actual floor the pulse-detection algorithm runs at. All three station scenarios use the same 6 dB margin; what differs is only the station's own noise floor (thermal + receiver noise figure for "quiet", rising with coax/power‑supply RFI for "typical" and "noisy"), consistent with the −52 to −80 dB relative‑unit range Motus operators report in the field.
  • The ~15 km figure holds up reasonably well as a rough station‑range number — but only in a fairly narrow band. Pick a terrain above and check the note under the table: a quiet station typically first reaches 15 km around a few hundred metres AGL, a typical station somewhat higher, and a noisy station rarely gets there at all. So "~15 km" is best read as roughly what a reasonably clean station can hear from a bird at a few hundred metres — not a number that applies uniformly at every altitude (a quiet station reaches over 100 km by 3000 m, see the table below).
  • Curves are jagged, not smooth — that's real, not noise in the calculation. They come from ground-reflection lobing in the antenna's elevation pattern: as altitude changes, the elevation angle at maximum range sweeps across a comb of lobes and nulls, so the outer edge of detectability can jump between adjacent lobes rather than varying smoothly. The signal-strength fields above show why — the underlying dBm surface itself is lobed, and each dashed contour just traces the outermost place it crosses a fixed threshold.
  • Near-ground tags (0–6 m, at or below the antenna's own mounting height) get a ground-roughness floor, not a raw null. A perfectly flat, homogeneous NEC2 ground plane predicts near-total destructive interference right at grazing incidence — but real terrain isn't a perfect mirror at that shallow angle, so it scatters diffusely instead of cancelling to near-zero. This model caps that null at −15 dB relative to boresight gain (see GROUND_ROUGHNESS_FLOOR_DB), which is why the quiet/typical/noisy curves go flat rather than to zero for altitudes 0–6 m: a tag sitting 1 m off the ground at a quiet station is still predicted detectable out to roughly 4 km, ~700 m at a typical station — consistent with community reports of several-hundred-metres-to-~1 km ground-level range (700–1200 m with smaller 5–6 element Yagis at lower mast heights). Treat the exact floor value as an estimate, not a measurement — real terrain roughness varies by site.
  • The radio horizon (dashed) is a hard physical ceiling from Earth curvature, not from signal strength — at these tag powers, link budget runs out long before line-of-sight does, so this line stays well beyond all three curves and rarely matters in practice.
  • No vegetation, terrain shadowing, or fuselage/body absorption loss is modelled beyond the ground-reflection effect already baked into each terrain's NEC2 pattern — real obstructed paths (forest canopy, hills) will fall well short of these numbers.
Link budget: Prx = Ptx + Gtag − FSPL(d, 166.38 MHz) + Grx(elevation, selected ground type, NEC2). Ptx=−10 dBm, Gtag=−6 dBi (0.1λ whip estimate). Elevation = atan2(altitude − 6 m, horizontal distance). Sensitivity = station noise floor + 6 dB fixed SNR. Radio horizon uses the 4/3‑Earth approximation, 4.12·(√h₁+√h₂) km. Ground types (average, desert, seawater, forest, freshwater, grassland) are separate NEC2 elevation-gain runs at the same antenna geometry.
